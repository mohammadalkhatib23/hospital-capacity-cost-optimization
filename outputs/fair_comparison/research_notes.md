# Research notes (Phase 1)

Question: how do researchers and practitioners compare forecasting methods fairly when the downstream decision has
asymmetric costs?

Written on 14 November 2026, before any backtest, forecast or score of this experiment. Every source below was opened.
"Read" says how much of it was read: the full text, part of the body, or the abstract only. Claims taken from an
abstract are limited to what the abstract says. Sources that could not be opened are listed at the end and are not
used as evidence.

## Summary of what the literature says

1. **Score the forecast the decision needs.** A point forecast is only "good" relative to a stated target. Absolute
   error rewards the median; the asymmetric piecewise linear ("pinball") score rewards a quantile. If the plan should
   sit at the 0.9 quantile, the 0.9 quantile is what must be forecast and scored (sources 7, 8, 9).
2. **Accuracy and decision value can disagree**, so studies report both: a statistical score and the cost of the
   decision the forecast leads to (sources 1, 4, 5, 6).
3. **Past forecast errors are the standard way to add protection.** Safety stocks, bootstrapped sample paths and
   conformal intervals all take a point forecast and add an empirical quantile of that method's own earlier errors
   (sources 10, 15, 16, 17).
4. **Small samples and time dependence limit what the tail can say.** With n earlier errors the 1 - alpha quantile is
   the ceil((n + 1)(1 - alpha))-th smallest error, so 0.9 needs at least 9 errors and 0.95 at least 19. The guarantee
   assumes exchangeable errors; errors from one series at overlapping horizons are not exchangeable (sources 16, 17,
   18).
5. **Hospitals usually plan to a risk level, not a shortage price.** Bed studies and NHS guidance use an overflow
   probability or an occupancy ceiling. Where a shortage or understaffing cost is used, it is assumed and varied, or
   inferred from behaviour; it is rarely measured (sources 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25).

## What does not transfer to a national monthly series

- **Formal coverage guarantees.** Conformal results need exchangeable calibration errors. Ours come from one series,
  from consecutive origins whose six-month targets overlap, and from a period that includes the recovery from the
  pandemic. Coverage is a hope, not a guarantee, and it must be reported (sources 16, 17, 18).
- **Queueing and daily-risk models.** Overflow probabilities and the bed shortage index are built from daily arrivals
  and lengths of stay in a unit or hospital (sources 20, 21). National monthly averages hide daily peaks and
  differences between trusts.
- **Decision-focused training.** Training a forecaster on decision loss needs many decision instances (sources 1, 2).
  We only evaluate on decision cost.
- **Measured costs.** No source gives a validated cost of a missing general and acute bed-month. The penalty stays an
  assumption (sources 23, 24, 25).
